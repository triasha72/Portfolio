independent · recommender systems · evaluation methodology

The articles it had never seen were the ones it ranked best.

NewsLens began with a narrower question than “build a news recommender”: how much of a recommender’s apparent quality survives once it is scored in the order the recommendations could actually have been made? Answering it put the evaluation protocol, rather than the model, at the centre of the project. It also produced a result I cannot yet explain — and the version of this project I am willing to defend is the one that says so.

At a glance

  • MIND-small: 51,282 articles, 156,965 impressions
  • 50,000 users, six days, CTR 4.04%
  • 125,572 train / 31,393 validation, chronological
  • Cutoff 2019-11-13 20:36:26, no temporal overlap
  • NDCG@10 0.2853 → 0.3594 → 0.3664
  • Paired bootstrap, 1,000 replicates, seed 42
  • v0.3.0, multi-platform image on GHCR

Research question

Given a user’s reading history, rank a candidate set of news articles. Microsoft’s MIND-small dataset supplies 156,965 impressions from 50,000 users across six days of November 2019, over a catalogue of 51,282 articles, at a click-through rate of 4.04%. The question is not which model wins. It is what an evaluation has to look like before “wins” means anything on data where every record carries a timestamp.

Why the split is the load-bearing part

A random interaction split lets Thursday’s behaviour inform a model scored on Tuesday’s recommendations. NewsLens splits chronologically at 2019-11-13 20:36:26 — 125,572 impressions before, 31,393 after — and refuses to separate records sharing an identical timestamp, which makes the requested 20% validation fraction a target rather than a guarantee. Here it lands at 20% exactly. The split object exposes an is_leakage_safe property asserting that the maximum training timestamp precedes the minimum validation timestamp, and the test suite fails if it does not.

Everything downstream is fitted on the training partition only: the popularity click counts, the TF-IDF vocabulary, and the article-engagement features exported from the DuckDB layer, which take an exclusive cutoff so that no validation-time event can enter a feature.

The result that does not sit right

That ordering is backwards. An article the training partition never once presented as a candidate should be the hard case. Instead it is the easiest one in the set, by a margin larger than the entire gap between the popularity baseline and the selected model.

What I have ruled out, and what I have not

The obvious explanation is that “unseen” means “no text.” It does not. Unseen means zero candidate appearances in the chronological training partition; the content model indexes all 51,282 articles regardless of whether any user was ever shown them, so a never-presented article arrives at scoring with exactly as much information as a heavily-presented one. That explanation is closed.

These are not. The exposure cohorts are not mutually exclusive — a single impression can contain clicked articles from several bands, so the four groups overlap and their shares do not sum to one. Publication recency is unmodelled and plausibly does the work: an article with zero training exposure is disproportionately likely to be new, and new articles may sit in smaller or less contested candidate sets. Heavily exposed articles may simply be the ones that appear in crowded lists. Nothing here is causal, and none of it is matched on category, time, or candidate-set size. The defensible statement is that the pattern is real in this data under this protocol and currently unexplained, which is what the repository’s open-questions document says.

The 927 impressions with no ranking at all

An aggregate metric hides abstention badly. The TF-IDF content model returned an empty top-10 for 927 of 31,393 validation impressions — 801 users with no history at all, and 126 whose history produced no positive similarity against any candidate. Those impressions score zero and are absorbed silently into the mean, where they read as a model that ranks poorly rather than a model that declined to answer.

Routing them to a popularity model fitted only on the training partition recovers all 927 and takes empty rankings to zero. The two score types are never blended: a TF-IDF cosine and a training click count are on incomparable scales, and averaging them yields a number with no interpretation. The router switches; it does not mix.

The intervals come from 1,000 paired resamples over 31,393 aligned impressions at seed 42, taking candidate minus baseline. All four exclude zero. That establishes the offline difference is not resampling noise, and it establishes nothing else — not online behaviour, not causal effect, not whether a reader would notice +0.007 of NDCG. Cold start is still the weakest history segment at NDCG 0.2954 after the fallback, which is the honest place to work next rather than a larger model.

What it takes to reproduce a ranking

A metric means little if the ranking behind it cannot be regenerated outside the process that produced it. The selected model exports as a versioned artifact: serialised model, typed metadata recording the training cutoff and parameters, and a SHA-256 manifest that rejects missing, corrupt, or unexpected files on load. A FastAPI service loads that artifact once at startup and keeps liveness and readiness separate — /health reports the process, /ready reports whether a verified artifact is actually serving and returns 503 when it is not, because a container that is running and a container that can answer are different claims.

The image is non-root, excludes both the licensed dataset and the artifact, and is published for amd64 and arm64. CI runs lint and the test suite, then builds the image, starts it with no artifact mounted, and asserts that it correctly reports itself unready.

What it does not settle

The official MIND development split has not been spent as a final untouched holdout, and the exposure result should be checked there before anyone believes it. TF-IDF captures shared vocabulary, not meaning. History articles are weighted equally regardless of recency. Popularity reflects what was shown at least as much as what was wanted. Subgroup confidence intervals are not reported, so a regression hidden inside the overall estimate would not be visible. No online experiment has been run, and no neural or transformer recommender has been trained — deliberately, because a more complex model is only justified once a simpler one has exposed a limitation it would fix.

Why this matters beyond news

Two habits transfer. The first is that a model which declines to answer looks identical, in an aggregate metric, to a model that answers badly — so abstention has to be counted separately or it disappears. The second is that the most interesting number in a study is often the one pointing the wrong way. The exposure result would have been easy to leave out; it is descriptive, it is confounded, and nobody reading the repository would have noticed it was gone. Publishing it as an open question is cheaper than discovering later that the evaluation was quietly measuring article age.
